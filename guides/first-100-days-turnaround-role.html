The first 100 days of a turnaround leadership role

A three-phase plan for leaders who start in a crisis: diagnose, stabilise, commit.

By the Glasscliff editorial teamUpdated 12 November 20264 minute readFor: Leaders starting a crisis role

In short

  • Spend days 1 to 30 learning and recording the position you inherited, not announcing a plan.
  • Days 31 to 60 are for stopping the bleeding and choosing no more than three priorities.
  • Days 61 to 100 are for committing: a 12-month plan, resources in writing, early wins shared widely.
  • Generate a tailored plan with the Turnaround 100-Day Planner.

Why the first 100 days matter more in a crisis

Every new leader is judged early. In a crisis, the judgement comes faster and with less patience, and atypical leaders tend to face more of it. The good news is that the first 100 days follow a pattern that works across most turnarounds. The plan below is the one our 100-Day Planner tailors to your situation.

Days 1 to 30: diagnose

The temptation is to show decisiveness with a big announcement in week one. Resist it. Early announcements made on incomplete information become the stick you are beaten with later.

  • Listen widely. Meet every direct report and at least twenty people two levels down. Ask what they would fix first and what they are afraid to say.
  • Get the real numbers. Cash runway, top customers, open legal and regulatory issues, key-person risks.
  • Write the state-of-play memo. A short, factual record of the position you inherited, shared with your board sponsor. If you negotiated an agreed baseline, this is where it becomes real.
  • Agree how you will report. Format, frequency and the handful of measures the board will watch.
  • Find two trusted insiders who will tell you what you are missing, especially if you were hired from outside.

Days 31 to 60: stabilise

  • Choose three priorities, and say no to the rest. A turnaround with ten priorities has none.
  • Stop the bleeding. Pause or end the activities that destroy the most value or cash.
  • Make the people decisions you have evidence for. Delay the ones you do not; acting on rumour damages trust you will need.
  • Set an operating rhythm. A weekly meeting, a single dashboard and one owner per priority.
  • Communicate the "why". People will accept hard decisions they understand more readily than easy ones they do not.

Days 61 to 100: commit

  • Present a 12-month plan with milestones that measure direction of travel, not just end states.
  • Lock in resources against that plan, in writing.
  • Share early wins and credit the people who delivered them.
  • Hold a private 100-day review with your sponsor: what changed, what you need next, what worries you.

How the plan changes by situation

SituationAdd in days 1–30Add in days 31–60
Financial distress13-week cash forecast; meet lenders earlyClear spending rules; focus on cash-generating customers
Reputational crisisMap stakeholders; one spokespersonFix the failure before relaunching the message
Toxic cultureConfidential listening; read exit interviewsAct visibly on one serious behaviour case
Post-scandalConfirm legal and disclosure dutiesCooperate with investigators; separate those implicated
Growth breaking the organisationFind bottlenecks; talk to lost customersSlow what growth is breaking; protect quality

Five mistakes to avoid

  1. Announcing a strategy before you have finished listening.
  2. Keeping quiet about the scale of the problem, so that it becomes yours.
  3. Trying to fix everything at once.
  4. Leaving your sponsor to hear bad news from someone else.
  5. Neglecting yourself. Crisis roles are long; protect sleep and keep one commitment outside work.

Questions

Should I announce my plan in the first week?

Generally no. In a crisis, use the first 30 days to diagnose and record the position you inherited. Announce priorities once you have evidence for them.

What is a state-of-play memo?

A short, factual record of the organisation's condition when you arrived, shared with your board sponsor. It protects you from being blamed for problems you inherited.

How many priorities should a turnaround have?

No more than three in the first 100 days. More than that spreads attention and resources too thin.
