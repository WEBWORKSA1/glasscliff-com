How to negotiate a turnaround role: the protections to ask for

The terms that separate a fair crisis appointment from a set-up, and how to ask for them.

By the Glasscliff editorial teamUpdated 12 November 20264 minute readFor: Leaders weighing an offer

In short

  • Your leverage is highest before you sign, when the organisation needs you most.
  • Negotiate the conditions for success first (mandate, resources, time, sponsor), then the protections if it fails (severance, indemnity).
  • Put everything that matters in writing: an offer letter, a mandate letter or a board minute.
  • Use the Offer Protection Builder to turn your risks into a prioritised list.

Why your leverage is highest now

An organisation in trouble that has chosen you has already invested time, money and reputation in the search. It wants a yes. After you start, that leverage disappears. This is why the most important negotiation of a turnaround role happens before day one, and why it should be about more than pay.

Think of the negotiation in two halves: the conditions that make success possible, and the protections that apply if it does not work out despite your best efforts.

Half one: the conditions for success

A mandate letter

A short document, signed by the chair or appointing body, covering your scope, decision rights, the problems you are being hired to solve, and how progress will be judged at 100 days, 12 months and 3 years. It protects you from a goalpost that moves after you arrive.

An agreed baseline

A written record of the condition you inherit: finances, legal exposure, operational issues, morale. Ask for it to be minuted by the board. You will be judged on the trend, and a trend needs a starting point.

Resources in writing

Budget, headcount and investment that the plan depends on, named in the offer letter or a board resolution. "We'll find the money" is not a resource.

A realistic time horizon

Agree interim milestones that measure direction of travel, and a formal review date that is no earlier than the time a reasonable plan needs. For most turnarounds that is 12 to 18 months, longer if culture change is involved.

Authority over your team

The right to make a defined number of senior appointments and exits in your first six months, with the budget to do it.

A named sponsor

One board member with real authority who meets you privately, often, in the first six months. Sponsorship is the single most consistent factor separating leaders who survive a difficult first year from those who do not.

Half two: protection if it goes wrong

Severance and change-of-control

Terms that apply if you are removed without cause, if the strategy you were hired to deliver is abandoned, or if the organisation is sold or merged. Have an employment lawyer review the definitions of "cause" and "good reason".

Indemnity and D&O insurance

Confirm in writing that directors' and officers' cover applies to you, including for decisions about problems that existed before you arrived.

Support you choose

A budget for an executive coach of your choosing for the first year, and if the role is public-facing, communications and security support.

For interim roles

Written criteria and a date for the decision about making the role permanent, and a completion payment if it ends.

How to ask without losing the offer

  1. Lead with the shared goal. "I want to succeed here, and these are the conditions I need to do that."
  2. Ask for the success conditions first. They cost the organisation little and show you are serious about the job, not just the exit.
  3. Bundle the protections. Present severance, indemnity and coaching as one standard package for a crisis role.
  4. Put it in writing. Follow every conversation with an email summarising what was agreed.
  5. Get independent advice. An employment lawyer and an adviser who has negotiated crisis roles will spot what you miss.
A reaction worth noticingHow an organisation responds to reasonable requests tells you how it will treat you when things get hard. A board that refuses to put the mandate in writing is telling you something.

Questions

Is it normal to ask for a mandate letter?

For senior and crisis roles, yes. Many experienced executives ask for one, and boards that are serious about the appointment usually agree.

Will asking for severance make me look like I expect to fail?

Presented as part of a standard package for a high-risk role, it signals that you understand the risks. Ask for the success conditions first.

Do I need a lawyer?

For any senior offer with severance, change-of-control or equity terms, an employment lawyer's review is worth the cost. This site does not provide legal advice.
