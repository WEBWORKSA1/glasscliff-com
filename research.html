Research Library

The key studies on the glass cliff, from the 2005 paper that named it to the newest work in 2025, including the studies that found no effect.

Studies

Each entry is summarised in our own words. Labels show whether a study supports the glass cliff, finds a nuanced result, or finds no effect.

The glass cliff: Evidence that women are over-represented in precarious leadership positions

Ryan & Haslam (2005). British Journal of Management, 16(2), 81–90.

SupportsThe study that named the phenomenon. FTSE 100 companies that appointed women to their boards had often experienced consistently poor share-price performance in the months before.

Find the paper

The glass cliff: Exploring the dynamics surrounding the appointment of women to precarious leadership positions

Ryan & Haslam (2007). Academy of Management Review, 32(2), 549–572.

SupportsA theoretical review setting out the possible explanations, from stereotypes to scarce opportunity, and a research agenda.

Find the paper

Legal work and the glass cliff

Ashby, Ryan & Haslam (2007). William & Mary Journal of Women and the Law, 13(3).

SupportsLaw students were more likely to assign a high-risk case to a female lead counsel.

Find the paper

The road to the glass cliff

Haslam & Ryan (2008). The Leadership Quarterly, 19(5), 530–546.

SupportsExperiments with management graduates, business leaders and students: women were seen as more suitable to lead organisations whose performance was declining.

Find the paper

Are female executives over-represented in precarious leadership positions?

Adams, Gupta & Leeth (2009). British Journal of Management, 20(1), 1–12.

No effect foundUS data: no evidence that women CEOs were appointed to firms in worse condition than men.

Find the paper

Politics and the glass cliff

Ryan, Haslam & Kulich (2010). Psychology of Women Quarterly, 34(1), 56–64.

SupportsIn the 2005 UK general election, women Conservative candidates were more likely to contest hard-to-win seats.

Find the paper

The glass cliff: When and why women are selected as leaders in crisis contexts

Bruckmüller & Branscombe (2010). British Journal of Social Psychology, 49(3), 433–451.

SupportsParticipants favoured a female candidate for a struggling company with a history of male leadership, suggesting she signalled change.

Find the paper

Think crisis–think female

Ryan, Haslam, Hersby & Bongiorno (2011). Journal of Applied Psychology, 96(3), 539–546.

SupportsStereotypically female traits matched what participants wanted in a leader of a failing company.

Find the paper

Glass cliffs and organizational saviors

Cook & Glass (2013). Social Problems, 60(2), 168–187.

SupportsMinority head coaches in US college basketball were more likely to be hired by losing teams and replaced by white coaches.

Find the paper

Above the glass ceiling: When are women and racial/ethnic minorities promoted to CEO?

Cook & Glass (2014). Strategic Management Journal, 35(7), 1080–1089.

SupportsFortune 500: women and minority executives more likely promoted to CEO of weakly performing firms, and more likely replaced by white men if performance declined.

Find the paper

From glass ceiling to glass cliff: Women in senior executive service

Sabharwal (2015). Journal of Public Administration Research and Theory, 25(2), 399–426.

Mixed or nuancedWomen in the US Senior Executive Service reported less organisational support and agency than men.

Find the paper

Leading at the top: Understanding women's challenges above the glass ceiling

Glass & Cook (2016). The Leadership Quarterly, 27(1), 51–63.

SupportsInterviews and data on women leaders: appointments in risky conditions, with less support and shorter tenures.

Find the paper

Getting on top of the glass cliff

Ryan, Haslam, Morgenroth, Rink, Stoker & Peters (2016). The Leadership Quarterly, 27(3), 446–455.

Mixed or nuancedA decade review: evidence, explanations, and why the effect is not universal.

Find the paper

The who, when, and why of the glass cliff phenomenon: A meta-analysis

Morgenroth, Kirby, Ryan & Sudkämper (2020). Psychological Bulletin, 146(9), 797–829.

Mixed or nuancedThree meta-analyses: a small archival effect that depends on domain; women more likely selected in crisis in experiments, more so in less gender-equal countries; extends to racial and ethnic minorities.

Exploring the glass cliff phenomenon in the appointments of female chairpersons

Goyal (2025). Coventry University report (British Academy / Leverhulme).

SupportsFTSE 350: higher likelihood of appointing female chairs in companies with sustained financial decline, especially during COVID-19.

Female CEO selection: Does the glass cliff exist?

Li et al. (2025). Summary by CourageUS.

No effect foundUS corporate data with several distress measures: no clear evidence of a glass cliff in CEO selection.

Women CEOs, tenure and dismissal risk

Social Science Quarterly (2025). Abstract via NYU Institute for Public Knowledge.

SupportsWomen CEOs had shorter tenures and higher dismissal risk, a gap that widened when appointed during weak performance.

Timeline

  1. 2003

    A newspaper article in The Times argues that women on boards are linked to weaker share prices.

  2. 2005

    Ryan and Haslam publish the FTSE 100 study and name the glass cliff.

  3. 2007

    Theoretical review in the Academy of Management Review; first evidence from legal work.

  4. 2008

    The New York Times lists the glass cliff among the ideas of the year.

  5. 2009

    Adams, Gupta and Leeth find no effect in US firms, opening the debate.

  6. 2010

    Evidence from UK politics and the 'signal of change' experiments.

  7. 2011

    'Think crisis, think female' published in the Journal of Applied Psychology.

  8. 2013–14

    Cook and Glass extend the pattern to minority leaders and describe the saviour effect.

  9. 2016

    'Glass cliff' is shortlisted for Oxford Dictionaries' Word of the Year; decade review published.

  10. 2020

    Meta-analysis in Psychological Bulletin finds a real but context-dependent effect.

  11. 2021

    Sophie Williams' TEDx talk brings the idea to a mass audience.

  12. 2025

    New studies on UK chairs, US CEO selection, CEO dismissal risk and ethnic minority leaders.
